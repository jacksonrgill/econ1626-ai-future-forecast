Prologue — 2024
Thesis

By 2027–2030, AI will bifurcate the accounting and financial advisory industry: compliance-heavy commodity work will be largely automated, concentrating productivity gains in large firms and platforms. The advisory relationship becomes more valuable — but harder to access at the mid-tier. Without active policy, this reinforces market concentration and locks displaced junior accountants out of the transition.

The wave is already here.

In 2013, Oxford researchers Frey and Osborne assigned accountants and bookkeepers a 94% probability of automation — the highest of any licensed profession. A decade later, the tools arrived. GPT-4-class models can draft tax returns, reconcile accounts, and sample audit populations faster than any junior accountant. The question is no longer whether AI reshapes accounting — it's how fast, who captures the gains, and who bears the cost.

Routine, codifiable tasks — data entry, payroll, basic tax returns — face near-total automation. Judgment-intensive advisory work — complex due diligence, dispute representation — remains far harder to replicate. This is the task framework of Autor, Levy & Murnane (2003) playing out in real time. The story plays out differently depending on where you sit. You are about to navigate it.

Uncertainty & Structural Break Risks
What could make all three scenarios wrong: These projections assume no AGI-level capability jump before 2030 and that the current regulatory pace holds. A step-change in AI reasoning capability would compress the upside/downside range dramatically. Conversely, a major AI liability or privacy incident could slow adoption across the board.
Data gaps: The most significant gap is granular task-level time-use data for Australian accounting roles. Frey & Osborne (2013) used US occupational definitions; Australian task profiles may differ, particularly for small-practice generalist accountants. The OECD (2023) acknowledges this methodological constraint explicitly.

Assumptions & Limitations

  • Employment projections are illustrative scenario estimates calibrated to published ranges — not point forecasts
  • Market share extrapolates from Australian IBISWorld data; global dynamics may differ materially
  • Assumes no AGI-level capability jump before 2030; structural break risk is not modelled
  • Policy timelines assume standard parliamentary and regulatory processes (~18–24 months)
  • Automation probability estimates (Frey & Osborne, 2013) pre-date GPT-4; actual displacement may be faster
